You are planning how to research a scientific goal, before any searching starts.

Research Goal: {{research_goal}}

Choose up to {{limit}} perspectives to research this goal from. A perspective is a distinct reason to go looking, not a subtopic, but a different question the goal raises about itself.

Instructions for perspectives:
1. Cover disagreement, not just support. Include the perspective most likely to find evidence against the goal's premise.
2. Keep perspectives independent: two perspectives that would issue the same searches are one perspective.
3. Name each as a short noun phrase, three words or fewer.
4. Prefer perspectives a specialist would recognize, such as mechanism, contradicting evidence, methodology, prior art, clinical translation or measurement limits, over invented framings.

For each perspective, ask the single most important question it needs settled about the goal. Each perspective funds one question and one search.

Instructions for questions:
1. Each question must be answerable from published literature and narrow enough that one search could find its answer.
2. Ask in this perspective's voice. A question any perspective would ask belongs to none of them.
3. Ask about the thing itself, such as mechanisms, magnitudes, populations, methods or failures, not about what the literature says in general.
4. Do not combine questions. Two things joined by "and" are two questions, and they get searched as one.

Turn each question into a query for a keyword index that requires every term to appear in a paper. Each extra word shrinks the result set, and a query that reads like a sentence returns nothing.

Instructions for queries:
1. Use 3-6 key terms separated by spaces. Never write a sentence or question.
2. Use established terminology a paper on this topic would use, including standard gene and protein symbols.
3. Omit quantitative details such as concentrations, fold-changes, timepoints and thresholds, and framing words such as "does", "is there evidence that", "novel" and "identify". They do not appear in indexed titles or abstracts and narrow the search toward zero.
4. Keep the question's subject. A query broad enough to always return something has stopped serving the question that produced it.

Good query: "AcrAB-TolC efflux pump inhibition carbapenem resistance"
Bad query: "Does inhibiting efflux pumps restore antibiotic sensitivity?"

Return fewer perspectives when the goal genuinely supports fewer. A perspective included to fill the list costs a full branch of searching. Do not invent questions or queries to fill a branch; omit a missing component so the existing focused planner can supply it.
